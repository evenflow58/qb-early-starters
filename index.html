Recruiting research · Classes of 2017–2024 · Pre-NIL vs. NIL era

Stars predict early starts — steeply.

Of every quarterback in the 247Sports Composite for 2022, 2023 and 2024, who started 6 or more games in a single season in either of his first two college seasons? The answer splits almost perfectly by star rating. Below, the same test is then run backwards — the pre-NIL classes of 2017–2021 — to test whether schools would now be wiser recruiting 3-star QBs instead of 4- and 5-stars. Verdict: partly supported — see “The hypothesis, tested” near the end. A final section then runs the same test at the other seven position groups (RB, WR, TE, OL, DL, LB, DB) — where the honest headline is that only verified floors, not full rates, could be established.

The headline in one line: A 5-star QB was about 10× as likely as a 3-star — and a 4-star about 3.4× as likely — to start 6+ games within his first two seasons. Yet because the 3-star pool is roughly five times larger, it still produced more early starters in absolute numbers (26) than the entire 4- and 5-star pool combined (22).

How the test was defined

Who counts

  • Classes 2022, 2023, 2024 — the three most recent classes to have fully completed their first two seasons as of Nov 2026 (2022 → 2022–23; 2023 → 2023–24; 2024 → 2024–25).
  • 247 Composite only, final rankings, QB position (pro-style + dual-threat combined).
  • Reclassified players sit in the class they ultimately signed in — e.g. Austin Simmons and Cutter Boley.
  • Star thresholds: 5★ ≥ .9834 · 4★ .8901–.9833 · 3★ .7901–.8900 · 2★ ≤ .7900.

What “starter” means here

  • 6+ games started in Year 1 or Year 2. Year 1 is the fall of the class year.
  • Transfers don’t reset the clock; starts across schools inside the window are summed.
  • Sports-Reference season tables don’t publish GS, so starts were taken from game logs and cross-checked against Wikipedia GP/GS tables, The Football Database, and official school bios — every qualifier and every 4–5-start near-miss individually verified.
  • Nothing was verified in a live browser session: 247Sports presented a bot challenge, so all figures are web-index research from 8 Nov 2026.

Follow-up: did the early starters stay?

Of the 48 early starters above (22 blue-chip + 26 three-star — the corrected qualifier set), how many were still at their school as of fall 2026? Two ways to count it, because five players transferred before they ever qualified and earned their starts at a second school.

Metric A — stayed at signing school

Never transferred away from the school he signed with. Leaving for the NFL draft or exhausting eligibility there counts as stayed. Any college transfer, whenever it happened, counts as not stayed.

Metric B — stayed at qualifying school

Did not transfer away from the school where he earned his 6+ qualifying starts. For Sayin, Demond Williams Jr., Holstein, Leavitt and Chiles — whose qualifying school differs from their signing school — this is judged on the qualifying school.

Transferred anyway — despite starting

  • Fernando Mendoza (2022, Cal → Indiana) — left Cal after two starting seasons, won the Heisman and a national title at Indiana in 2025, drafted No. 1 overall in 2026. A transfer that “worked,” but a transfer under both metrics.

The mirror study · Classes of 2017–2021

Before NIL: the same test, run backwards

NIL took effect July 1, 2021. Roughly the same span that has passed since then reaches back to the classes of 2017–2021 — every one of them signed before NIL existed. This section applies exactly the same definitions as the post-NIL study above: 247 Composite final rankings, high-school QBs only (JUCO excluded), pro-style + dual-threat combined, early starter = ≥6 starts in a single season in Year 1 or Year 2, starts summed across schools, reclassified players counted in the class they signed in.

The hypothesis, tested

The claim: “In the NIL era, schools would be wise to recruit 3-star QBs with high upside and low downside instead of 4- and 5-star QBs they don’t benefit from.” Judged component by component against both eras:

OVERALL · PARTLY SUPPORTED
Right about who delivers starts in-house. Wrong about loyalty and upside.

The hypothesis is right that, in the NIL era, the early-starter production a signing school actually keeps is much closer between 3-stars and blue chips than recruiting rankings imply — because 3-stars match blue chips in raw early-starter output and are captured at ~96–100% while blue-chip capture has slipped to ~77%. It is wrong on its two supporting premises: 3-star early starters are not more loyal in the NIL era (their retention fell furthest), and 3-star upside is not comparable per player (draft rate ~22% vs. ~53%; first-round rate ~6% vs. ~29% of early starters).

“For getting a quarterback on the field early and keeping his qualifying season in-house, a portfolio of 3-star QBs now delivers nearly what blue-chips deliver; for keeping that quarterback for his whole career or getting an NFL-caliber player, star level still matters a great deal.”

(i) Raw counts SUPPORTED

In both eras, 3-stars produced more early starters per class than all blue chips combined (pre-NIL 10.2 vs. 7.6 per class; post-NIL 8.7 vs. 7.3), despite a per-recruit hit rate roughly one-quarter of the blue-chip rate (~7% vs. ~26%) — simply because the 3-star pool is ~5× larger. Even on the conservative floor (~40 verified pre-NIL 3-star qualifiers = 8.0/class), the counts are comparable.

The qualification that matters for roster math: a school signing several 3-star QBs should expect roughly as many early starters as a school signing the same number of blue chips only if it signs ~4× as many of them — the raw-count parity is a pool-size effect, not a per-player effect.

(ii) Capture SUPPORTED

Pre-NIL: 3-star capture 96.1% vs. blue-chip 89.5% (2017–2020: 97.5% vs. 93.8%). Post-NIL: 3-star capture a perfect 26/26 vs. blue-chip 17/22 (77.3%).

The era change is concentrated exactly where the hypothesis says: blue-chip capture fell ~16 points from the clean pre-NIL classes to the NIL classes — driven by elite QBs (Sayin-type cases post-NIL; Fields, Ewers and Dart pre-figuring it) taking their qualifying seasons elsewhere — while 3-star capture did not move. This is the strongest component of the hypothesis.

(iii) Retention PARTLY SUPPORTED

Direction: yes — Metric A fell from 32.9% (pre-NIL, final careers) to 22.9% (post-NIL, a snapshot that can only decline), and Metric B from 39.0% to 27.1%. But the star-level pattern cuts against the hypothesis: the drop was larger for 3-stars (Metric A 29.5% → 15.4%) than for blue chips (36.8% → 31.8%). Whatever makes early starters leave in the NIL era hits the supposed “low downside” group hardest — consistent with 3-star starters transferring up for money and stage (Penix, Hartman, Gabriel and Leonard all left their qualifying schools pre-NIL too).

And pre-NIL retention was already poor: two-thirds of pre-NIL early starters transferred at least once, because the portal (2018) and graduate transfers predate NIL. The NIL-era drop is an acceleration, not a regime change from loyalty to mobility.

(iv) Upside NOT SUPPORTED

As stated — “3-stars with high upside” as a general claim — the pre-NIL draft data does not support it. Among pre-NIL early starters, 52.6% of blue chips were drafted vs. 21.6% of 3-stars; 5-star early starters went 9/11 drafted with eight first-rounders.

The 3-star successes are genuine — Zach Wilson (#2), Michael Penix Jr. (#8), Kenny Pickett (#20), Brock Purdy, Desmond Ridder, Dillon Gabriel — but the median 3-star early starter was an undrafted G5 starter (40 of 51 were never drafted), and several high-production 3-star starters (Sam Hartman, Kedon Slovis, Grayson McCall) went undrafted entirely. “High upside” describes the top tail of the 3-star distribution, not the typical 3-star early starter. A 3-star strategy buys early starts and capture reliability; it does not buy equivalent NFL-caliber upside per starter produced.

Why “NIL did it” is only partly identifiable — confounders

The timeline problem

  • Transfer portal launched October 15, 2018; graduate transfers with immediate eligibility long predate it.
  • One-time free-transfer rule adopted April 2021 (ratified April 28, effective for 2021–22).
  • NIL took effect July 1, 2021.
  • August 2020: the NCAA granted all fall athletes a blanket extra year of eligibility for the COVID season.

So the “pre-NIL” era is not a pre-mobility era: classes 2019–2021 were recruited and played entirely after the portal existed, and even the 2017–2018 classes made most stay/leave decisions after October 2018. Many pre-NIL transfers were graduate transfers (Hartman, Brewer) or waiver cases (Fields) that needed no NIL and no free-transfer rule. Pre-NIL Metric A of ~33% is the single most important context number in this study: mobility was already the norm.

What can and can’t be attributed

  • Retention contrasts conflate three simultaneous changes (portal 2018, free transfer April 2021, NIL July 2021) plus COVID eligibility. The free-transfer rule plausibly matters more than NIL for the median QB — it removed the sit-out year, the binding constraint — while NIL money concentrates at the top of the market. Nothing in this design can separate the April 2021 rule from the July 2021 rule for the 2022–24 classes.
  • COVID eligibility lengthened careers and multiplied transfer opportunities for the 2017–2021 classes specifically (e.g. Max Johnson starting at a fourth school in a seventh season in 2026; Hartman’s sixth-year move). That inflates pre-NIL transfer counts — so the true pre/post retention gap from the 2021 rule changes is, if anything, larger than the raw gap, but pre-NIL careers are not a clean “normal-times” baseline either.

The cleanest comparisons, ranked

  • (a) Early-starter production rates — cleanest. For the 2017–2019 classes, the Year 1/Year 2 windows predate free transfer entirely, and rankings predate NIL by construction. The near-identity of hit rates across eras (blue ~26–29% across the era cuts; 3-star ~6–7% in both) is the study’s most trustworthy finding — and it says recruiting rankings did not lose predictive power.
  • (b) Capture. The qualifying moment is early-career, so capture contrasts (especially blue-chip 93.8% → 77.3%) are only lightly contaminated, mainly for the 2019–2020 classes whose Year 2 fell in 2020–21.
  • (c) 2021 transition class vs. 2022–24. Both played under portal + free transfer + NIL; they differ mainly in whether NIL existed at signing. The transition class’s intermediate Metric A (28.6%, between 33.8% and 22.9%) hints at a modest signing-time NIL effect on top of the transfer-rule effect.
  • (d) Career retention — least clean, and it should carry the least weight in the verdict — which is why component (iii) is only “partly supported.”

Extension · The other positions

Does the QB pattern hold at RB, WR, TE, OL, DL, LB and DB?

The same definitions were applied at the other seven position groups — RB; WR; TE; OL (OT + IOL/OG + C); DL (interior DL + EDGE/DE); LB (ILB + OLB); DB (CB + S) — across both eras. ATH-designated recruits were excluded unless 247’s position listing placed them in one of the groups (so A.J. Dillon, listed as an ATH in 2017, is out of RB, and Malaki Starks, an ATH in 2022, is out of DB; Sonny Styles, listed at S, is in DB, and Travis Hunter, listed at CB, is in DB). Kickers, punters and long snappers were excluded.

The honesty headline — read this first: unlike QB, no full census was possible at these positions, and this section does not pretend otherwise. A complete count would mean individually evaluating roughly 2,800 blue-chips plus screening roughly 10,000 three-stars. Three documented blockers prevented it: 247Sports’ live site blocked the research browser with a client challenge; the web index exposes only the first ~50 names of each Composite list, so exact per-position pool counts could not be extracted; and Sports-Reference CFB’s indexed player tables frequently omit the GS column entirely, so starts had to come player-by-player from school bios and GP–GS tables. What follows is therefore a verified floor — every early starter for whom both the Composite star level and the starts figure are sourced — plus “not tracked” (NT) wherever a number could not be verified. No early-starter rates are presented for non-QB positions: any rate computed from these floors would divide a floor numerator by an unknown denominator.

How to read the floor numbers

The floor samples are selected on prominence — a player enters the floor because a school bio, media guide or national article documented his starts. Prominence correlates with staying at one school and with being drafted, which is why the floor-sample capture and retention figures sit at ~100%: they are ceilings on plausibility, not estimates. The QB census (retention 32.9% pre-NIL) proves a full census finds the transfers and non-draftees a prominence floor misses. Do not quote the non-QB sample percentages as rates.

Confidence grades, by position

  • WR — Medium. Multiple qualifiers verified with exact GS in both eras and both star bands; still a floor.
  • OL — Medium (as a floor). One class-wide screen (2022) plus official-bio GS; rates are lower bounds by construction.
  • RB — Medium-low. Qualifiers verified, but GS is a distorted metric at RB and at least one major case (Judkins) could not be GS-verified.
  • DB — Medium-low. Strong verified cases both eras; the post-NIL 3-star list is likely incomplete beyond South Carolina’s pair.
  • DL — Low. Rotation makes GS sparse; one verified qualifier per era.
  • LB — Low. One verified qualifier total (Perkins, post-NIL); pre-NIL LB is not tracked.
  • TE — Low. One verified qualifier (Bowers, transition class); the best-documented 3-star case is a non-qualifier; post-NIL TE starts largely unverified.

What the verified evidence does support

3-star early starters exist everywhere — and reached the top

The verified pre-NIL 3-star floor (5) actually exceeds the verified pre-NIL blue-chip floor (4): Jonathan Taylor (RB, 2017), Deuce Vaughn (RB, 2020), Justin Jefferson (WR, 2017), Rashawn Slater (OL, 2017) and Sauce Gardner (DB, 2019), against Penei Sewell, Peter Skoronski, Kayvon Thibodeaux and Brock Bowers. That mirrors the QB pool-size pattern in direction — but the floors are prominence-selected and cannot be converted into rates.

Pre-NIL, the verified 3-stars were drafted at least as impressively

Jefferson (R1 #22), Slater (R1 #13) and Gardner (R1 #4) were first-rounders, Taylor a 2nd-rounder and Vaughn a 6th-rounder — 5 of 5 drafted — against 4 of 4 blue-chips drafted, all in Round 1. That is the opposite shape from the QB upside gap (blue 52.6% vs. 3-star 21.6%). Because this sample is selected on prominence, which correlates with being drafted, the population draft-rate comparison remains cannot tell.

The clearest post-NIL retention failure is a blue-chip

Caleb Downs (5-star S, Alabama 2023) started all 14 games as a freshman, then transferred to Ohio State in January 2024 after Nick Saban’s retirement. In the verified post-NIL floor, 11 of 12 early starters never transferred from their signing school; Downs is the sole exception. That does not replicate the QB finding that the retention drop was largest for 3-stars — but the floor is far too small and biased to overturn it either.

Starts are a position-distorted metric

This confounder did not exist at QB. Kayvon Thibodeaux led Oregon in sacks (9) as a true freshman while starting only 5 games (rotation). Nick Singleton “started” only 8 of 13 as a freshman while rushing for 1,061 yards in a two-back rotation. Quinshon Judkins ran for 1,567 yards as a freshman at Ole Miss, yet his Sports-Reference page as indexed does not even expose a GS column. Starts undercount early RB/DL impact in a way they do not for QB, OL or DB.

Instructive non-qualifiers & exclusions

Sam LaPorta — the definition bites at TE

LaPorta (3★, Iowa, 2019) started 2 games in Year 1 and 5 in Year 2 — 7 cumulative, but not 6+ in a single season (and Iowa played only 8 games in 2020), so he is not an early starter under this study’s exact definition, despite becoming Iowa’s starter in Year 3 and a 2nd-round NFL pick. Cumulative-start counting would misclassify exactly the developmental 3-star TEs the strategy debate is about.

Quinshon Judkins — the case a census must catch

Judkins (3★, Ole Miss, 2022) ran for 1,567 yards as a freshman, but his GS could not be verified from indexed sources, so he is excluded from the counts — and he later transferred to Ohio State, making him exactly the capture/retention case a completed census would need to resolve. Ollie Gordon II (2022, Oklahoma State), described as a three-star signee, is likewise excluded: his Year-2 starter status is documented narratively but exact GS was not sourced.

OL: early starts are a scarcity event

Phil Steele’s screen of the top-50 OL recruits of 2022 found only four full-season starters as true freshmen in the entire class — Campbell (13), Banks (13), Jones (12) and Clemson’s Blake Miller (14; star level unresolved in this pass) — and only seven OL recruits who earned any start at all. Even at the very top of the OL pool, a Year-1 starting job is a top-of-class event; for the median blue-chip OL, starts arrive in Year 3+. All OL numbers here are floors / lower bounds.

DB: the cleanest paired contrast

Capture for Downs is 100% — every qualifying start came at his signing school — yet retention is 0%: Alabama developed a Year-1 star and lost him before Year 2 for a reason (a legendary head coach’s retirement) that has nothing to do with NIL money per se. Meanwhile the two verified post-NIL 3-star DBs at a non-blueblood (South Carolina’s Emmanwori, plus Smith flagged) both stayed. A single-program anecdote, not a rate — but it shows the QB study’s capture/retention divergence operating at DB in its purest form.

Per-position verdicts — does the QB pattern hold?

The QB pattern’s four components: (i) 3-stars produce more early starters per class than blue chips (pool-size effect); (ii) signing-school capture fell post-NIL for blue chips (93.5% → 77.3%) while 3-star capture stayed ~96–100%; (iii) career retention of early starters was already low pre-NIL (33.3%) and fell post-NIL (22.9%), with the drop largest for 3-stars (29.5% → 15.4%); (iv) pre-NIL NFL upside among early starters was much higher for blue chips (52.6%) than 3-stars (21.6%).

Overall synthesis

OVERALL · NO SINGLE TRANSPLANT
The QB verdict does not transplant as one verdict — it splits by position.

At RB and DB, the raw-production leg is at least as strong as at QB and the upside leg looks weaker; at WR, the raw-production leg reverses post-NIL in the floor evidence; at OL and TE, all four legs are dominated by development curves that push value outside the measurement window; at DL and LB, the measurement itself is not yet adequate. A program acting on the QB study alone would over-apply the strategy at WR, mis-time it at OL/TE, and have no evidence at DL/LB.

Where the strategy generalises

  • The pool-size premise is real at RB and DB. In both eras, verified 3-star early starters at RB (Taylor, Vaughn, Jeanty) and DB (Gardner, Emmanwori) reached the very top of the sport — a #4 overall draft pick, a #6 overall pick — without transferring. A program signing many 3-star RBs/DBs is buying many lottery tickets at positions with short development curves and heavy rotation, where Year-1/2 opportunity is genuinely available. That is the QB pattern’s strongest export.
  • Retention risk post-NIL is not a 3-star-specific phenomenon outside QB. The one verified post-NIL retention loss in this entire extension is a 5-star (Downs), driven by a coaching change. Roster-building that assumes “blue chips stay, 3-stars leave” (or the reverse) is not supported by anything verified here; the QB study’s 3-star retention collapse (29.5% → 15.4%) should be treated as a QB finding until a census shows otherwise.

Where it breaks down

  • WR (post-NIL): the verified early starters are blue chips starting as true freshmen at Missouri, Arizona, Ohio State and Alabama. At a position where the best 17–18-year-olds now start immediately and are visible doing so, “recruit 3-stars for volume” does not reproduce the QB arithmetic in the floor evidence — the portal half of the strategy (buy proven starters) looks relatively stronger at WR, and homegrown volume looks weaker.
  • OL and TE (both eras): developmental positions. Early starts are scarce events — four full-season true-freshman starters among 2022’s top-50 OL recruits; LaPorta needing until Year 3 despite an NFL trajectory. For OL/TE, the Years-1–2 window measures almost nothing about a signing class’s eventual value, and a 3-star-volume strategy cannot be evaluated — or executed for early returns — on this clock. OL/TE value concentrates in Years 3–5, which is precisely the window the portal can also buy. The honest conclusion is not “3-stars win” or “blue chips win” but “early-starter metrics are the wrong instrument here.”
  • DL and LB: the instrument, not the strategy, fails. Rotation (DL) and data absence (LB) mean this study cannot say whether the QB pattern holds. Any roster conclusion for DL/LB drawn from GS-based early-starter counts — in either direction — would be unfounded on current evidence.

Position-specific confounders

New in this extension

  • GS is not a uniform currency. It is near-complete information for OL and DB, partial for WR/TE (formation-dependent), and actively misleading for RB (series-rotation “starts”) and DL (package rotation).
  • Early NFL entry at RB/DB compresses careers to three years: “never transferred” is easier to satisfy, and post-NIL retention snapshots for the 2024 class (e.g. Smith, Williams — both still at their signing schools in fall 2026) are provisional, since neither has yet faced a draft/portal decision point after Year 3.
  • Coaching-change transfers (Downs, after Saban’s retirement) are retention failures that correlate with blue-chip status — blue chips cluster at bluebloods, whose coaching changes are the highest-profile — rather than with NIL bidding alone. Capture/retention analyses that do not control for head-coach departure will misattribute these.
  • Composite-vs-service star confusion is a live data hazard at exactly the margins that matter (Jeanty, Graham, Singleton — see above).
  • FCS signings in the pool (Hunter at Jackson State, and others) sit inside the main Composite lists; their starts are the least documented of all, biasing any floor against small-school signees of both star bands.

Carried over from the QB study

  • Transfer portal launched October 15, 2018 — even “pre-NIL” classes 2019–2021 played with a portal, and 2017–2018 classes gained one mid-career.
  • One-time free-transfer rule, April 2021 (ratified April 28, 2021) and NIL from July 1, 2021 — the 2021 class signed pre-NIL but played entirely under both, hence its transition flag; Bowers (2021T) is retained in the pre-NIL mirror for comparability and flagged everywhere.
  • COVID eligibility and shortened seasons — the extra eligibility year stacked rosters with super-seniors in 2021–2022 (suppressing Year-1/2 starts for the 2021 and 2022 classes), and the shortened 2020 season compressed the GS ceiling: Iowa played 8 games in 2020 (LaPorta’s maximum possible GS was 8; he recorded 5), Oregon played 7 (Thibodeaux started all 7), Kansas State 10 (Vaughn started 7). A 6+ GS threshold is a materially higher bar in a 7–10-game season than in a 13–15-game season, biasing era comparisons against the pre-NIL mirror’s later classes.
